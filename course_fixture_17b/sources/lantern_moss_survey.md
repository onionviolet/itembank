# Lantern moss survey, Aldrasse fen

Synthetic source for the 17B tracer. Lantern moss (given here the
invented binomial Lucernaria aldrassensis) and every observation below
are fictional. Heading slugs are the stable locator anchors; cite
sections as `lantern_moss_survey.md#<slug>`.

## Species description

Lantern moss is a brown moss of mineral-rich fens, named for the pale,
translucent capsule that swells at the tip of each fertile stem in late
summer and appears to glow when backlit at a low sun angle. Vegetative
shoots form dense, springy cushions two to five centimeters tall, deep
olive above and rust brown below, with tightly overlapping concave
leaves that hold capillary water between them. The species is a
calciphile: cushions establish only where porewater calcium stays above
roughly eight milligrams per liter, which confines it to laggs and
seepage tracks and makes it a reliable indicator of lateral mineral
inflow. In the Aldrasse transect it is the visual signature of the
minerotrophic condition, and its absence from an otherwise suitable
hollow is the first field clue that seepage there has failed.

## Annual cycle

The survey year divides into four observable phases. In spring, cushions
green up within days of ice-out and begin extension growth while the
water table is at its annual high. Early summer is the investment phase:
shoots that sit within two centimeters of the capillary fringe initiate
fertile stems, while drier shoots skip reproduction and bank the season
as vegetative growth. Late summer brings the lantern phase, when
capsules swell, blanch, and release spores during the first dry
northerly winds of September. Autumn is the wager phase: the cushion
commits its stored sugars to a final flush of rhizoid growth that anchors
it against winter ice heave. The cycle's hinge is the early-summer water
position. A cushion that misreads it, initiating capsules in a season
the table then abandons, loses both the capsules and the growth it
deferred, and the survey's plot records show such double losses take a
cushion two full seasons to recover.

## Hydrological sensitivity

Plot data from nine seasons make the species' water dependence usable as
an instrument. Cushion vitality scores track the mean summer water table
with a lag of about one month, and the relationship is asymmetric:
cushions tolerate brief flooding with no measurable cost, but any
four-week period with the table more than twenty five centimeters down
produces browning from the cushion crown outward. Because that threshold
matches the drought state defined in the hydrology notes, lantern moss
condition can stand in for a dipwell where none is installed. The survey
protocol scores five cushions per station on a four-point vitality scale,
and a station mean below 2.5 is treated as equivalent to a recorded
drought excursion for monitoring purposes.

## Conservation status in the Aldrasse basin

Within the basin the species is secure but narrowly held: eleven of the
fourteen mapped colonies sit inside the lagg arc fed by the western
ridge, so a single interception of that seepage, one drainage ditch or
one widened road cut, could strand the majority of the population above
its calcium floor. The survey therefore treats colony count as a
secondary signal and seepage integrity as the primary one. Management
recommendations in this source are deliberately modest: maintain the
ridge woodland that meters the recharge, keep machinery off the lagg arc,
and resample any colony within one season of a conductance alarm at
station A1.
