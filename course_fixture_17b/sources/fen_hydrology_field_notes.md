# Fen hydrology field notes, Aldrasse basin

Synthetic source for the 17B tracer. The Aldrasse basin, its stations,
and every figure below are invented. Heading slugs are the stable locator
anchors; cite sections as `fen_hydrology_field_notes.md#<slug>`.

## Basin overview

The Aldrasse fen occupies a shallow glacial trough between two low
moraine ridges. Unlike a rain-fed bog, the fen is minerotrophic: its
water arrives laterally, seeping from the ridge aquifers, and carries
dissolved calcium and magnesium picked up from the till. That mineral
supply is the single most consequential fact about the system. It buffers
the porewater near a pH of 6.2 to 6.8, it feeds a sedge and brown moss
community rather than a Sphagnum lawn, and it sets the boundary
conditions for every process described in these notes. Field stations
A1 through A9 run in a transect from the western ridge toe to the central
flark, spaced at forty meter intervals, each with a dipwell, a crest-stage
tube, and a marked peat-depth probe point.

## Water table dynamics

The water table in the Aldrasse fen behaves like a damped oscillator
driven by two inputs on different clocks. Seasonal recharge from ridge
seepage raises the table slowly through autumn and winter, while
individual storm events produce sharp spikes that decay over three to
five days. The damping comes from the peat itself: the upper, loosely
decomposed layer (the acrotelm) has a hydraulic conductivity two to three
orders of magnitude higher than the dense catotelm below, so storm water
moves laterally through the acrotelm and exits at the flark margin before
it can pressurize the deeper profile. Dipwell records from stations A3
and A7 show the practical consequence: summer drawdown rarely lowers the
table more than eighteen centimeters below the peat surface, and any
excursion past twenty five centimeters marks a drought state the
vegetation registers within a fortnight. When the table sits within the
acrotelm, the fen sheds water quickly and stays wet; when it drops into
the catotelm, the fen holds what it has and dries slowly from the top.

## Peat accumulation and decay

Peat forms where plant production outruns decay, and in the Aldrasse fen
that race is decided by oxygen. Above the water table, aerobic microbes
decompose fresh litter within a few seasons. Below it, decay continues
anaerobically but at perhaps one hundredth the aerobic rate, so material
that survives its transit through the oxygenated zone is effectively
banked. The accumulation rate measured against the dated ash layer at
station A5 averages just under one millimeter per year, which is
ordinary for a temperate fen and still remarkable: three meters of
profile under the central flark represents roughly three thousand years
of banked carbon. The practical rule the transect data support is that
accumulation tracks the position of the water table, not the productivity
of the surface. A wet year with modest growth banks more peat than a
lush dry year, because the decay term moves more than the production
term.

## Mineral inflow and the lagg boundary

Where the ridge seepage first enters the fen, a transitional lagg zone
forms: wetter, more mineral-rich, and floristically distinct from both
the ridge forest and the open fen. The lagg matters for two reasons.
First, it is the fen's chemical gatekeeper: calcium delivered there
precipitates or is taken up before water reaches the interior, so the
central flark runs measurably leaner than station A1. Second, it is the
early-warning line. Ditching, road salting, or fertilizer runoff show up
in lagg porewater chemistry seasons before the interior community shifts.
Station A1's specific conductance record is therefore read first in any
disturbance assessment, and a sustained rise above two hundred
microsiemens per centimeter there triggers a full transect resample under
the monitoring protocol in these notes.
