# Every second counts

Original synthetic teaching material, not a bank, graded sitting, or learner evidence.

## Same start, different speeds

Mira walks at 3 metres per second. Noor walks at 5 metres per second.
Both start at the same place and time, walk in the same direction, and keep
their speed. Predict how far apart they will be after 6 seconds before
reading the worked example.

## Reference R1: distance, speed, and time

Distance = speed × elapsed time. A speed of 3 m/s means adding 3 metres every
second. The units explain why the rule works: (metres / second) × seconds
leaves metres.

This is a finite constant-speed model. Different starting points, changing
speed, or opposite directions need different assumptions and calculations.

## Worked example at 6 seconds

1. Mira: 3 m/s × 6 s = 18 m.
2. Noor: 5 m/s × 6 s = 30 m.
3. Gap: 30 m − 18 m = 12 m.

Noor travels 2 more metres than Mira every second, so the gap also follows
(5 − 3) × time = 2 × time. Changing time moves both walkers and changes the
gap; it never changes either walker's speed.

| Time | Mira | Noor | Gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 s | 0 m | 0 m | 0 m |
| 4 s | 12 m | 20 m | 8 m |
| 6 s | 18 m | 30 m | 12 m |
| 12 s | 36 m | 60 m | 24 m |

A distance-versus-time graph contains two straight lines through the origin.
Mira's line has slope 3; Noor's has slope 5. At 6 seconds the vertical
distance between the lines is 12 metres. Noor's steeper line means a greater
speed, not more elapsed time.

## Try a new time

At 4 seconds, predict the gap. Then compare with the model:
5 × 4 − 3 × 4 = 20 − 12 = 8 metres. This is a public worked demonstration,
not a scored response.

## Keep the idea

Explain why the gap grows by the same amount each second. A useful starting
sentence is: "In the same one second…"
